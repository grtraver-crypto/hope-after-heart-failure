# Hope After Heart Failure's Daily Assessment and Monitoring Log

*A daily tracking tool to share with your healthcare team*

This log is designed for people recently diagnosed with heart failure, and for the caretakers who love them. Fill it out once a day, at roughly the same time, and bring it (or share it electronically) with your cardiologist, heart failure nurse, or primary care provider at each visit. Consistent daily tracking is one of the most effective ways to catch early warning signs before they become an emergency.

## Why this matters

This is new territory for you. There is suddenly a lot of information to take in. Very important information that you need to become intimately aware of. I know from experience how frustrating (and infuriating) it can be to try to keep track of it all. I put this cheat sheet together to make the process a bit easier to understand. This is also useful to our caretakers, because they are often as confused as we are. What you will gain from this is peace of mind. You get the information written down and you don't have to second guess yourself if you checked your blood pressure or weighed yourself. Wondering a few hours later if I had taken meds has caused needless stress and anxiety. Relax, if it crosses your mind you have your daily log handy.

> This resource is educational and does not replace advice from your own healthcare team. Always follow the specific instructions, targets, and thresholds your doctor or heart failure nurse gives you — they may differ from the general guidance below based on your individual condition.

## How to Use This Log

- Weigh yourself every morning after using the bathroom and before eating, on the same scale, wearing similar clothing.
- Check your blood pressure and pulse at roughly the same time each day, seated and rested for 5 minutes first.
- Fill in every row daily, even if the answer is 'no change' — trends matter more than any single day.
- Bring this log (or a photo/copy of it) to every appointment, and call your care team sooner if you notice any of the warning signs listed in the sections below.
- If you experience chest pain, severe shortness of breath at rest, fainting, or think you may be having a medical emergency, call 911 (or your local emergency number) — do not wait to log it first.

## What Each Item Means and Why It Matters

The items below are listed in the same order they appear in the Weekly Tracking Table above, so you can move between the log and these explanations quickly. Every item is still worth tracking daily, regardless of order.

### 1. Daily body weight

**Why it matters:** Sudden weight gain is usually fluid, not fat, and is often the earliest sign that heart failure is getting worse — sometimes appearing days before you feel short of breath or swollen. Weighing yourself the same way every morning (after the bathroom, before breakfast, same scale, similar clothing) lets you and your care team catch fluid buildup early, before it sends you to the hospital.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- A gain of 2–3 lbs in one day
- A gain of about 5 lbs in a week
- Steady weight gain over several days even if each day's change seems small

*Sources: Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032; American Association of Heart Failure Nurses (AAHFN). Daily Weights patient education sheet, aahfn.org*

### 2. Blood pressure

**Why it matters:** Many heart failure medications (like ACE inhibitors, ARNIs, and beta-blockers) change your blood pressure, and your care team uses your home readings to adjust doses safely. Blood pressure that runs too low can cause dizziness or falls; blood pressure that runs too high can strain the heart further.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- Readings consistently below 90/60 or your care team's personal low-end target, especially with dizziness or lightheadedness
- Readings consistently above the target your care team has given you
- A big change from your usual pattern in either direction

*Sources: Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032; Lee S, et al. Effectiveness of a Smartphone App-Based Intervention With Bluetooth-Connected Monitoring Devices in Heart Failure (SMART-HF Trial). J Med Internet Res. 2024;26:e52075*

### 3. Heart rate / pulse

**Why it matters:** Your heart rate helps show how hard your heart is working and whether medications that slow the heart rate (like beta-blockers) are dosed correctly. A pulse that's unusually fast, slow, or irregular can be an early sign of a rhythm problem, which is common alongside heart failure.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- Resting heart rate consistently above 100 or below 50 beats per minute (unless your care team has told you a different target)
- A new irregular, fluttering, or racing heartbeat
- Heart rate changes paired with dizziness, chest discomfort, or fainting

*Sources: Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032; Lee S, et al. Effectiveness of a Smartphone App-Based Intervention With Bluetooth-Connected Monitoring Devices in Heart Failure (SMART-HF Trial). J Med Internet Res. 2024;26:e52075*

### 4. Swelling (edema) in legs, ankles, or belly

**Why it matters:** Like weight gain, swelling is a visible sign of fluid retention. It commonly shows up in the lowest parts of the body (ankles, feet, lower legs) or the abdomen, and tends to get worse throughout the day.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- New or worsening swelling in your legs, ankles, or feet
- Your belly feeling more swollen or tight, or your clothes/shoes fitting tighter
- Swelling that leaves a dent when you press on it and doesn't bounce back quickly

*Sources: Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032; American Heart Association. Managing Heart Failure Symptoms. heart.org (reviewed 2024)*

### 5. Fatigue and energy level

**Why it matters:** Unusual tiredness or a drop in how much activity you can do without stopping to rest is one of the more subtle warning signs, because it can be mistaken for 'just getting older' or being out of shape. Rating it daily helps separate a normal tired day from a real downward trend.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- Feeling unusually exhausted doing tasks that used to be easy
- Needing to rest more often during normal daily activities
- A sudden, unexplained drop in energy over a few days

*Sources: Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032; American Heart Association. Managing Heart Failure Symptoms. heart.org (reviewed 2024)*

### 6. Mood and mental state

**Why it matters:** Depression and anxiety are very common after a heart failure diagnosis — far more common than in the general population — and they make it harder to stick with medications, diet, and monitoring. Guidelines now recommend routinely screening for depression using simple questionnaires (PHQ-2, then PHQ-9 if needed), because treating mood problems improves both quality of life and a patient's ability to self-manage.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- Persistent sadness, hopelessness, or loss of interest in things you used to enjoy
- New or worsening anxiety, worry, or panic
- Trouble sleeping, concentrating, or wanting to withdraw from family and friends
- Any thoughts of self-harm — this needs immediate attention

*Sources: Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032; Rutledge T, et al. Anxiety and Depression in Heart Failure: An Updated Review. Prog Cardiovasc Dis. 2023*

### 7. Medication taken as prescribed

**Why it matters:** Heart failure medications work best when taken consistently and exactly as directed — skipping doses (even unintentionally) is one of the most common causes of a preventable hospital readmission. A simple daily yes/no check helps you and your care team spot patterns, like missed doses on busy days.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- Missed doses, even occasional ones
- Side effects that make you want to stop or skip a medication on your own
- Confusion about which medications to take or when

*Sources: Meraz R, et al. Resilience mediates the effects of self-care activation and hope on medication adherence in heart failure patients. Res Nurs Health. 2023;46(3):323-335; The use of technology to improve medication adherence in heart failure patients: a systematic review of RCTs. J Pharm Policy Pract. 2023; Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032*

### 8. Shortness of breath / breathing changes

**Why it matters:** Fluid backing up into the lungs makes breathing harder — first with activity, then possibly at rest or while lying flat (needing extra pillows to sleep, or waking up gasping for air). Tracking this daily on a simple 0–3 scale helps your team see a trend, not just a bad day.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- Needing more pillows than usual to breathe comfortably at night
- Waking up suddenly short of breath
- Getting winded with activities that didn't used to bother you
- Shortness of breath at rest — this needs urgent attention

*Sources: Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032; American Heart Association. Managing Heart Failure Symptoms. heart.org (reviewed 2024)*

### 9. Sodium and fluid intake

**Why it matters:** Extra salt makes the body hold onto more water, and heart failure already makes it harder to get rid of excess fluid — so what you eat and drink directly affects your weight, swelling, and breathing. Current expert guidance favors a moderate, consistent sodium intake (roughly at or under 2–3 grams per day, per your care team's specific target) over extremely strict restriction, which hasn't been shown to improve outcomes and can backfire.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- Eating notably saltier food than usual (restaurant meals, canned/processed foods, added salt)
- Drinking more fluid than your care team recommends, especially if you've been told to limit fluids
- Increased thirst alongside weight gain or swelling

*Sources: Mullens W, et al. Dietary sodium and fluid intake in heart failure: a clinical consensus statement of the Heart Failure Association of the ESC. Eur J Heart Fail. 2024;26(4):730-740; Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032*

### 10. Physical activity level

**Why it matters:** Regular, appropriately-paced physical activity (as approved by your care team, often through cardiac rehabilitation) improves heart failure symptoms and quality of life. Tracking what you did and how you tolerated it helps your team fine-tune your activity plan and catch a declining tolerance for exercise early.

**Contact your care team if you notice:**

- Being unable to do your usual activity or exercise without excess breathlessness or fatigue
- New chest discomfort, dizziness, or palpitations with activity
- A noticeable, sustained drop in how far or how long you can walk

*Sources: Heidenreich PA, et al. 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. Circulation. 2022;145(18):e895-e1032; The use of technology to improve medication adherence in heart failure patients: a systematic review of RCTs. J Pharm Policy Pract. 2023*

## When to Call Your Care Team vs. When to Seek Emergency Care

### Call your heart failure team the same day if you notice:

- Weight gain of 2-3 lbs in one day, or about 5 lbs in a week
- Increasing swelling in your legs, ankles, or abdomen
- Increasing shortness of breath with activities that didn't used to bother you
- Needing more pillows to sleep, or waking up short of breath
- New or worsening fatigue that limits your normal activities
- A new irregular, fast, or unusually slow heartbeat
- Questions about a missed medication dose or side effects

### Seek emergency care (call 911) if you experience:

- Severe shortness of breath at rest, or a feeling of suffocating
- Chest pain or pressure
- Fainting or feeling like you're about to pass out
- A very fast or irregular heartbeat with dizziness or chest discomfort
- Coughing up pink, frothy mucus

## Heart Failure Medications Glossary

Below are the medication classes most commonly prescribed for heart failure, listed by drug class with common generic and brand names. Not everyone is prescribed every medication — your combination depends on your type of heart failure, other health conditions, and how you respond to treatment. This list covers currently FDA-approved medications; it does not include drugs still in clinical trials that aren't yet approved for general use. Because heart failure is often preceded by another cardiac condition — coronary artery disease, high blood pressure, or an irregular heart rhythm — this list also includes a few common medications for those related conditions near the end, even though they don't treat heart failure directly. Never stop or change a dose without talking to your care team first.

## Glossary of Heart Failure Terms

Plain-English definitions of medical terms you're likely to hear from your care team, read in your discharge paperwork, or see in this log.

| Term                                                              | Plain-English meaning                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Heart failure (HF)                                                | A condition where the heart muscle doesn't pump blood as well as it should, causing fluid to build up in the lungs, legs, or abdomen and leaving the body without enough oxygen-rich blood.                         |
| Congestive heart failure (CHF)                                    | An older term for heart failure, still used interchangeably by many doctors and hospitals; it emphasizes the fluid ('congestion') buildup that heart failure causes.                                                |
| Cardiomyopathy                                                    | Disease of the heart muscle itself that makes it weaker, stiffer, or abnormally shaped, and is a common underlying cause of heart failure.                                                                          |
| Ischemic cardiomyopathy                                           | Heart muscle damage caused by reduced blood flow, usually from coronary artery disease or a past heart attack.                                                                                                      |
| Non-ischemic cardiomyopathy                                       | Heart muscle damage from a cause other than blocked arteries, such as viral infection, genetics, chemotherapy, alcohol, or long-term high blood pressure.                                                           |
| Ejection fraction (EF)                                            | A percentage measuring how much blood the left side of your heart pumps out with each beat. Used to classify heart failure as reduced (HFrEF), mildly reduced, or preserved (HFpEF) EF.                             |
| HFrEF                                                             | Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction — the heart's main pumping chamber squeezes weakly (EF ≤40%).                                                                                                        |
| HFmrEF                                                            | Heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (EF 41-49%) — a middle category between HFrEF and HFpEF.                                                                                                       |
| HFpEF                                                             | Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction — the heart squeezes normally but doesn't relax and fill properly (EF ≥50%).                                                                                       |
| Systolic dysfunction                                              | A problem with the heart's squeezing (pumping) ability — the hallmark of HFrEF.                                                                                                                                    |
| Diastolic dysfunction                                             | A problem with the heart's ability to relax and fill with blood between beats — the hallmark of HFpEF.                                                                                                             |
| Preload                                                           | The amount of blood stretching the heart's chambers just before it contracts; affected by how much fluid is in the body.                                                                                            |
| Afterload                                                         | The resistance the heart must pump against to push blood out to the body, largely driven by blood pressure and blood vessel stiffness.                                                                              |
| Cardiac output                                                    | The amount of blood the heart pumps per minute; a key measure of how well the heart is meeting the body's needs.                                                                                                    |
| Stroke volume                                                     | The amount of blood pumped out of the heart with a single beat.                                                                                                                                                     |
| Edema                                                             | Swelling caused by fluid building up in body tissue, commonly seen in the legs, ankles, feet, or abdomen in heart failure.                                                                                          |
| Pitting edema                                                     | Swelling that leaves a temporary dent when pressed with a finger — a classic sign of fluid retention.                                                                                                              |
| Anasarca                                                          | Severe, widespread swelling throughout the body, seen in advanced or poorly controlled heart failure.                                                                                                               |
| Ascites                                                           | Fluid buildup in the abdominal cavity, which can cause belly swelling, bloating, or tight-fitting clothes.                                                                                                          |
| Pulmonary edema                                                   | Fluid buildup in the lungs that causes severe shortness of breath and can be a medical emergency.                                                                                                                   |
| Congestion                                                        | A medical term for fluid buildup in the lungs or body caused by the heart not pumping efficiently; this is what causes swelling and breathlessness.                                                                 |
| Euvolemic                                                         | A state of normal, balanced fluid levels in the body — the goal of heart failure treatment.                                                                                                                        |
| Hypervolemia                                                      | Having too much fluid in the body, which worsens heart failure symptoms like swelling and breathlessness.                                                                                                           |
| Orthopnea                                                         | Shortness of breath that happens when lying flat, usually relieved by sitting up or using extra pillows.                                                                                                            |
| Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND)                                | Sudden, severe shortness of breath that wakes you from sleep, caused by fluid shifting into the lungs while lying down.                                                                                             |
| Dyspnea                                                           | The medical term for shortness of breath or difficulty breathing.                                                                                                                                                   |
| Rales / crackles                                                  | Abnormal crackling sounds heard in the lungs with a stethoscope, often caused by fluid; something your care team listens for at visits.                                                                             |
| Jugular venous distension (JVD)                                   | Visible bulging of the neck veins that can indicate too much fluid and pressure in the heart and blood vessels.                                                                                                     |
| S3 gallop                                                         | An extra, abnormal heart sound your doctor may hear with a stethoscope that can suggest fluid overload or a weakened heart.                                                                                         |
| Cachexia                                                          | Unintentional weight loss and muscle wasting that can occur in advanced or long-standing heart failure.                                                                                                             |
| Hepatomegaly                                                      | An enlarged liver, which can happen when heart failure causes blood to back up into the liver.                                                                                                                      |
| Arrhythmia                                                        | Any abnormal heart rhythm — too fast, too slow, or irregular.                                                                                                                                                      |
| Atrial fibrillation (AFib)                                        | A common irregular, often rapid heart rhythm that frequently occurs alongside heart failure and raises stroke risk.                                                                                                 |
| Tachycardia                                                       | A heart rate that is faster than normal (generally over 100 beats per minute at rest).                                                                                                                              |
| Bradycardia                                                       | A heart rate that is slower than normal (generally under 60 beats per minute), which can sometimes be caused by heart failure medications.                                                                          |
| Hypotension                                                       | Low blood pressure, which can cause dizziness or fainting and is a common side effect of several heart failure medications.                                                                                         |
| Hypertension                                                      | High blood pressure, a major risk factor for developing and worsening heart failure.                                                                                                                                |
| Hyponatremia                                                      | Low blood sodium levels, which can occur in heart failure and sometimes requires fluid restriction to correct.                                                                                                      |
| Hyperkalemia                                                      | High blood potassium levels — a possible side effect of some heart failure medications (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI, MRAs) that requires monitoring with blood tests.                                               |
| Renal function / kidney function                                  | How well the kidneys are filtering waste from the blood; closely linked to heart failure and monitored regularly because many HF medications affect the kidneys.                                                    |
| GFR (Glomerular Filtration Rate)                                  | A blood-test-based estimate of how well your kidneys are working, tracked over time in heart failure care.                                                                                                          |
| Creatinine                                                        | A waste product measured in blood tests to estimate kidney function; levels can shift with heart failure medications and need monitoring.                                                                           |
| BNP / NT-proBNP                                                   | A blood test measuring a hormone released when the heart is under strain; higher levels can indicate worsening heart failure.                                                                                       |
| Anemia                                                            | A lower-than-normal red blood cell count, common in heart failure patients and a cause of extra fatigue.                                                                                                            |
| Sleep apnea                                                       | A sleep disorder involving pauses in breathing during sleep that is common in heart failure and can worsen symptoms if untreated.                                                                                   |
| Echocardiogram (echo)                                             | An ultrasound of the heart used to measure ejection fraction and check heart structure and valve function.                                                                                                          |
| Electrocardiogram (EKG/ECG)                                       | A quick test that records the heart's electrical activity to check heart rhythm and detect certain problems.                                                                                                        |
| Cardiac catheterization                                           | A procedure where a thin tube is guided into the heart's blood vessels or chambers to check for blockages, measure pressures, or take tissue samples.                                                               |
| Stress test                                                       | A test that checks how the heart performs under physical exertion (or medication that mimics exertion), often used to look for blocked arteries.                                                                    |
| Six-minute walk test                                              | A simple test measuring how far you can walk in six minutes, used to track functional capacity and treatment response over time.                                                                                    |
| Chest X-ray                                                       | An imaging test that can show fluid in the lungs, heart size, and other changes related to heart failure.                                                                                                           |
| Cardiac MRI                                                       | A detailed imaging scan of the heart sometimes used to identify the specific cause of cardiomyopathy.                                                                                                               |
| Valve regurgitation                                               | A heart valve that doesn't close tightly, allowing blood to leak backward; can contribute to or worsen heart failure.                                                                                               |
| Valve stenosis                                                    | A heart valve that has narrowed or stiffened, restricting blood flow and increasing strain on the heart.                                                                                                            |
| NYHA Class (I-IV)                                                 | A scale (New York Heart Association) describing how much your symptoms limit physical activity, from Class I (no limitation) to Class IV (symptoms at rest).                                                        |
| ACC/AHA Stage (A-D)                                               | A staging system describing heart failure progression, from Stage A (at risk, no disease yet) to Stage D (advanced, treatment-resistant disease).                                                                   |
| GDMT (Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy)                         | The combination of evidence-based medications (such as ACE inhibitors/ARNI, beta-blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors) recommended for most heart failure patients to improve survival and reduce hospitalizations. |
| Titration                                                         | The gradual, step-by-step adjustment of a medication dose (often upward) to reach the most effective and best-tolerated level.                                                                                      |
| Decompensation                                                    | A sudden worsening of heart failure symptoms, often due to fluid overload, that may require urgent treatment or hospitalization.                                                                                    |
| Comorbidity                                                       | Another health condition (such as diabetes, kidney disease, or high blood pressure) that occurs alongside heart failure and can affect its management.                                                              |
| Palpitations                                                      | A sensation of a racing, pounding, fluttering, or irregular heartbeat.                                                                                                                                              |
| Polypharmacy                                                      | Taking multiple medications at once, which is common in heart failure care and requires extra attention to interactions and side effects.                                                                           |
| Angioedema                                                        | Rare but serious swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat that can occur with ACE inhibitors and requires immediate medical attention.                                                                         |
| ACE inhibitor cough                                               | A dry, persistent cough that some people develop on ACE inhibitors, which usually resolves after switching to a different medication class (such as an ARB).                                                        |
| Cardiac rehabilitation ('cardiac rehab')                          | A supervised program of exercise, education, and support for people with heart conditions, shown to improve symptoms and quality of life.                                                                           |
| Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD)                      | A small device placed under the skin that monitors heart rhythm and delivers a shock to correct dangerous, life-threatening rhythms.                                                                                |
| Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) / biventricular pacemaker | A specialized pacemaker that helps the heart's chambers beat in a more coordinated way, used in certain types of heart failure.                                                                                     |
| Left ventricular assist device (LVAD)                             | A mechanical pump implanted to help a severely weakened heart circulate blood, used for advanced heart failure.                                                                                                     |
| Heart transplant                                                  | Surgery to replace a severely failing heart with a healthy donor heart, reserved for the most advanced cases.                                                                                                       |
| Telehealth / remote monitoring                                    | Using phone, video, or connected devices (like Bluetooth scales or blood pressure cuffs) to share health data with your care team without an in-person visit.                                                       |
| Fluid restriction                                                 | A care-team-directed limit on how much liquid you drink per day, sometimes used to help control fluid buildup.                                                                                                      |
| Sodium restriction                                                | Limiting dietary salt intake to help the body avoid holding onto extra fluid.                                                                                                                                       |
| DASH diet                                                         | 'Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension' — an eating pattern rich in fruits, vegetables, and low-fat dairy that's often recommended alongside sodium limits.                                                      |
| PHQ-2 / PHQ-9                                                     | Short standardized questionnaires used to screen for depression; PHQ-2 is a quick 2-question screen, and PHQ-9 is a more detailed 9-question follow-up.                                                             |
| Self-care/self-management                                         | The everyday actions patients take — like monitoring symptoms, taking medications, and adjusting diet or activity — to manage their own condition between medical visits.                                         |
| Advance directive                                                 | A legal document stating your wishes for medical care if you're unable to speak for yourself, often discussed as part of heart failure planning.                                                                    |
| Palliative care                                                   | Specialized medical care focused on relieving symptoms and improving quality of life at any stage of a serious illness — not the same as end-of-life care.                                                         |
| Hospice care                                                      | Care focused on comfort rather than cure, typically for patients with advanced illness and a limited life expectancy.                                                                                               |
